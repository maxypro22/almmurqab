How end-of-service gratuity is calculated in Qatar

The statutory minimum in Qatar is three weeks' pay for each year of service, calculated on your last basic wage. Here is how that works in practice.

End-of-service gratuity is among the most common questions we are asked, and among the most commonly miscalculated. The difficulty is rarely the law itself, which is clear. It is which number you start from.

What the law says

Article 54 of Qatar's Labour Law No. 14 of 2004 governs this entitlement. It sets one straightforward rule: a worker who has completed one year or more of service is entitled, on termination, to a gratuity of not less than three weeks' pay for each year of employment.

Statutory minimum
3 weeks' wage per year of service
Minimum service
One completed year
Calculation basis
Last basic wage
Partial years
Paid pro rata

The point most people miss

The calculation runs on the last basic wage — not on total pay. Allowances (housing, transport, and similar) fall outside the basis unless the contract says otherwise. That single distinction can halve the figure an employee was expecting.

Working it out

Take the last monthly basic wage and divide it by thirty to get a daily rate. Multiply that by twenty-one days — three weeks — and you have the entitlement for one year. Multiply by the years of service, counting any part-year proportionally.

An example: a worker whose last basic wage is QAR 9,000, with four and a half years of service. The daily rate is QAR 300, so one year is worth QAR 6,300, and four and a half years comes to QAR 28,350. That is the statutory floor, not a ceiling.

A contract or company policy may grant more than the minimum, and many do. The law sets only the floor; where the contract is more generous, the contract governs.

Continuity of service

One point matters for anyone who left and later rejoined the same employer: service is treated as continuous where the worker returns to work within two months of termination, other than in the cases set out in Article 61. The earlier years are not written off.

Where disputes arise

  • Disagreement over what counts as "basic wage" where the contract is vague about the split between wage and allowances
  • Deductions taken against alleged worker liabilities with no proper basis
  • Calculating on an older wage rather than the last basic wage
  • The claim that resigning forfeits the entitlement — which is not the general rule
  • Payment delayed past the end of the employment relationship without justification

Before you sign a release

Do not sign a final release or settlement before you are satisfied the figure is right. Signing can weaken your position later even where the calculation was wrong. Check the numbers first, or have a lawyer check them.
